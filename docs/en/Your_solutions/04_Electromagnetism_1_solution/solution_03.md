

**Short Explanation:**
For the third charge to be in electrostatic equilibrium, the net force acting on it must be zero. Since $q_1$ and $q_2$ are both positive, they will both repel the positive $q_3$ charge. For $q_3$ to sit completely still, the repulsion force from $q_1$ pushing it to the right must perfectly equal the repulsion force from $q_2$ pushing it to the left. 

Here is the step-by-step solution:

### **1. Set Up the Variables**
*   Let **$x$** be the distance from $q_1 (+4 \text{ C})$ to $q_3$.
*   Since the total distance between $q_1$ and $q_2$ is $2 \text{ m}$, the distance from $q_3$ to $q_2 (+9 \text{ C})$ must be **$(2 - x)$**.

### **2. Set the Forces Equal**
Using Coulomb's Law, the magnitude of the force from $q_1$ on $q_3$ ($F_1$) must equal the magnitude of the force from $q_2$ on $q_3$ ($F_2$).

$$F_1 = F_2$$

$$k \frac{q_1 \cdot q_3}{x^2} = k \frac{q_2 \cdot q_3}{(2 - x)^2}$$

### **3. Simplify the Equation**
Because Coulomb's constant ($k$) and the charge $q_3$ appear on both sides of the equation, we can cancel them out. This proves that the magnitude of the test charge ($+1 \text{ C}$) actually doesn't matter for finding the equilibrium position!

$$\frac{q_1}{x^2} = \frac{q_2}{(2 - x)^2}$$

Plug in the values for $q_1$ and $q_2$:

$$\frac{4}{x^2} = \frac{9}{(2 - x)^2}$$

### **4. Solve for $x$**
To get rid of the squared terms, take the square root of both sides of the equation:
$$\frac{2}{x} = \frac{3}{2 - x}$$

Now, cross-multiply to solve for $x$:

$$2(2 - x) = 3x$$

$$4 - 2x = 3x$$

Add $2x$ to both sides:

$$4 = 5x$$

Divide by $5$:

$$x = \frac{4}{5} = 0.8 \text{ m}$$

**Final Conclusion:** The charge $q_3$ must be placed $0.8 \text{ m}$ from the $+4 \text{ C}$ charge.
